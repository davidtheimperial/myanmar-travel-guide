Culture of Myanmar

Myanmar's culture is not one tradition but many, layered over centuries and shared across more than a hundred recognised ethnic groups. Religion, food, dress and festival calendars all overlap, and the result is a country where a single street can hold a monastery, a tea shop and a mosque.

By David Gaius · Updated 26 November 2026

In short

Myanmar's culture blends Theravada Buddhism with more than a hundred ethnic groups. Expect a lunar festival calendar, the longyi as everyday dress, and food that mixes Indian, Chinese and Thai influences. At religious sites, cover shoulders and knees and remove both shoes and socks.

Peoples and languages

The Bamar form the largest group, but Shan, Karen, Rakhine, Chin, Kachin, Mon, Kayah and many smaller communities each have their own languages, clothing, music and customs. Burmese is the official language and the common second language across the country; in Shan State you will hear Shan and Chinese as readily as Burmese, and English is widely taught.

This diversity is one of the reasons the country's politics are so complicated. Many ethnic armed organisations have fought for autonomy for decades, and the 2021 coup pushed several of them into open alliance with the pro-democracy resistance. The history section traces how the modern state took shape.

Religion and daily life

Theravada Buddhism shapes the rhythm of ordinary life for most Bamar, Shan, Mon and Rakhine people. Monks are given the place of honour at meals and on buses, and many men ordain temporarily as novices at least once in their lives. Merit-making — offering food to monks, releasing fish, gilding a pagoda, lighting candles — is a routine part of the week rather than a special occasion.

Christianity, Islam, Hinduism and various traditional beliefs are also practised, particularly among Chin, Kachin and Karen communities and in the cities. You will also see thanaka, a pale paste ground from bark and worn on the face as sun protection and decoration.

Festivals

The festival year follows the lunar calendar, so dates shift against the Gregorian calendar each year. Thingyan, the Water Festival, is the biggest: it marks the Myanmar New Year in mid-April with days of water-throwing, music and merit-making. Thadingyut, the Festival of Lights, marks the end of Buddhist Lent in October with lanterns and candlelit pagodas, and Tazaungdaing in November brings offerings of new robes to monks — and, in Taunggyi, a famous hot-air balloon festival.

What people wear

The longyi is the everyday garment: a cylinder of cloth wrapped at the waist and tucked in, worn by men and women alike. Men's versions are usually plain or checked; women's are more colourful and are often paired with a fitted blouse. On formal and religious occasions the outfit is completed with a taikpon jacket for men, or a shawl for women. In cities, trousers and jeans are common among younger people, but the longyi remains standard at offices, markets and monasteries.

Food

Myanmar's cooking borrows from India, China and Thailand without quite resembling any of them. Mohinga — a fish-based noodle soup thickened with chickpea flour — is the unofficial national dish, eaten for breakfast from street stalls and home kitchens alike. Lahpet thoke (tea leaf salad) mixes fermented tea leaves with peanuts, fried garlic, sesame and tomato, and is served at the end of a meal as often as the start.

Shan noodles, curries served with rice and a spread of side dishes, and the pickled-tea culture that goes with them are all worth seeking out. Food is usually shared, and the strongest flavours are balanced with plain rice rather than toned down.

Music and dance

Traditional music is built around the saung, a boat-shaped harp, and the hsaing waing, a drum-and-gong ensemble that drives open-air theatre and festival performances. Dance is narrative rather than abstract: performers tell stories from the Jataka tales, history and myth, and each ethnic group has its own distinct styles and instruments.

Arts and crafts

Lacquerware from Bagan, wood carving, bronze casting, silk and cotton weaving, and gold leaf beaten by hand in Mandalay are all living trades rather than tourist demonstrations. Many workshops welcome visitors, and buying directly from makers is one of the more useful ways to spend money in a country where tourism income is unevenly distributed. The attractions page covers Bagan and Mandalay in more detail.

Visiting with respect

  • Remove shoes and socks before entering a pagoda, monastery or someone's home.
  • Cover shoulders and knees at religious sites; a longyi or sarong borrowed at the entrance is fine.
  • Do not touch anyone on the head, and avoid pointing your feet at people or Buddha images.
  • Ask before photographing monks, worshippers or anyone at prayer. Some sites ban photography of the images themselves.
  • Women should not touch monks or hand items directly to them; place offerings on a cloth or table instead.
  • Avoid political discussion with strangers unless they raise it first.
