History of Myanmar

Myanmar, formerly Burma, has been inhabited for thousands of years and ruled as a kingdom, a colony, a socialist republic and a military state. This is a short version of a very long story — enough to make sense of the country's monuments and its present politics.

Timeline

  1. c. 2nd century BCE – 9th century CE

    Pyu city-states

    Walled cities such as Sri Ksetra and Halin traded with India and China and left behind the earliest surviving urban culture in the Irrawaddy valley.

  2. 849

    Pagan is founded

    A small settlement on a bend of the Irrawaddy grows into the capital of the first Burmese empire.

  3. 1044–1077

    Anawrahta and the golden age

    Anawrahta unifies the valley, adopts Theravada Buddhism and begins the temple-building programme that still defines Bagan's skyline.

  4. 1287

    Pagan falls

    Mongol invasions break the empire apart. Power fragments into competing Shan and Burmese states for the next two centuries.

  5. 1510–1752

    Toungoo dynasty

    Bayinnaung briefly assembles the largest empire in Southeast Asian history, stretching from Manipur to Laos.

  6. 1752–1885

    Konbaung dynasty

    The last Burmese monarchy expands west, moves the capital to Mandalay, and reforms the state under King Mindon before colliding with British India.

  7. 1824–1885

    Three Anglo-Burmese Wars

    Britain takes increasingly large slices of the country, ending with the annexation of the whole kingdom in 1885.

  8. 1886–1948

    British rule

    Burma is governed as part of British India until 1937, then as a separate colony. Roads, railways and rice exports expand while resistance smoulders in the countryside.

  9. 4 January 1948

    Independence

    Burma becomes independent under Prime Minister U Nu, with a parliamentary system and a promise of autonomy for frontier peoples that is never fully delivered.

  10. 1962

    Ne Win's coup

    General Ne Win seizes power, closes the country to the outside world and launches the "Burmese Way to Socialism", which bankrupts one of Asia's richest economies.

  11. 1988

    The 8888 Uprising

    Student-led protests spread nationwide and are crushed by the army. A new military junta takes over; Aung San Suu Kyi emerges as the opposition's figurehead.

  12. 1990

    A result that was ignored

    The National League for Democracy wins a landslide election. The military annuls the result and holds Suu Kyi under house arrest for most of the next two decades.

  13. 2011–2021

    Reform, then reversal

    The generals begin a managed transition: sanctions ease, the NLD wins again in 2015, and Aung San Suu Kyi becomes state counsellor. Reforms stall, and the army's role in politics remains entrenched.

  14. 1 February 2021

    The coup

    The military detains the elected government, declares a state of emergency and triggers nationwide protests, a nationwide civil-disobedience movement, and eventually armed resistance.

  15. 2021 – now

    A country at war

    The resistance has grown into a broad alliance of People's Defence Forces and ethnic armed organisations, and large areas are no longer under military control. The human cost is the subject of the travel advisory.

"Burma" or "Myanmar"?

Both are used, and the choice carries political weight. The military government renamed the country "Myanmar" in 1989, along with Yangon (formerly Rangoon) and many other places. The UN and most governments adopted the new name; some opposition groups and media outlets still prefer "Burma" to avoid legitimising the regime that introduced it. In practice, most publications now use "Myanmar" neutrally, as this site does.

The Pagan Empire

Bagan is the country's defining historical landscape: over 2,000 surviving temples, monasteries and stupas built largely between the 11th and 13th centuries on a plain beside the Irrawaddy. The architecture shows the meeting of Mon, Pyu, Indian and later Chinese influences, and the city was a major centre of Buddhist scholarship until the Mongol invasions of the 1280s.

Restoration has been controversial. A 1990s rebuilding campaign used modern materials and built new structures on old foundations, which is part of why the site's UNESCO listing (2019) came so late and with conditions attached.

Colonial rule and independence

The three Anglo-Burmese Wars (1824–26, 1852, 1885) stripped the Konbaung monarchy of its territory piece by piece. Under British rule, rice exports boomed and Rangoon became one of the great ports of Asia, but the economy was built for export rather than for the people living in it. Nationalist movements grew between the wars, and Burma was separated from British India in 1937.

Independence came on 4 January 1948, largely through negotiation led by Aung San — assassinated the previous year, and the father of Aung San Suu Kyi. The new union was federal in name but centralised in practice, and several ethnic armies took up arms within a few years.

Isolation and the 1988 uprising

Ne Win's 1962 coup ended parliamentary politics for half a century. Foreign businesses were nationalised, the currency was repeatedly demonetised, and the country slid from being one of Asia's most promising economies to one of its poorest. In 1988, protests that began among students in Rangoon spread to almost every town before the army opened fire. The junta that followed renamed itself the State Law and Order Restoration Council and later the State Peace and Development Council.

Reform, 2011–2021

A new constitution in 2008 reserved a quarter of parliamentary seats for the military. The 2010 elections were boycotted by the NLD and widely criticised, but the government that took office in 2011 surprised observers by freeing political prisoners, easing censorship and opening the economy. The NLD won a landslide in 2015, and for five years the country had a civilian government sharing power uneasily with the army — including over the Rohingya crisis in Rakhine State, which drove hundreds of thousands of people into Bangladesh.

Since the 2021 coup

The coup reversed a decade of opening in a single morning. Civil disobedience paralysed hospitals, schools and banks; protests were met with lethal force; and by 2022 armed resistance had spread across much of the country. By 2024, resistance forces and ethnic armed organisations had taken significant territory, including major border crossings and towns in Shan and Rakhine states, leaving the military in its weakest position in decades.
