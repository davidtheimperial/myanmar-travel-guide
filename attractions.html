Attractions in Myanmar

Ancient temples, a lake farmed on floating islands, a gilded stupa that dominates Yangon's skyline and a long stretch of quiet coast. These are the places most often asked about — and the ones most worth understanding before you go.

Five places to start with

The highlights

Each entry covers what the place actually is, rather than what a brochure would say. Distances and days are rough planning estimates only.

Ancient capital

Bagan

The plain beside the Irrawaddy holds more than 2,000 surviving temples, stupas and monasteries, most built between the 11th and 13th centuries. Sunrise over the brickwork is the cliché, and it earns it.

Mandalay Region · UNESCO World Heritage since 2019 · allow 2–3 days

Lake

Inle Lake

A shallow lake in Shan State where gardens are grown on rafts of weed, villages sit on stilts, and fishermen row standing up with one leg wrapped around the oar. Boat trips run to markets, monasteries and weaving workshops.

Shan State · base in Nyaungshwe · allow 2–3 days

Sacred site

Shwedagon Pagoda

The most sacred Buddhist site in the country, a 99-metre stupa covered in gold leaf on a hill above central Yangon. Go at dusk, when the marble platform fills with families and the gold catches the last light.

Yangon · open daily · allow 2–3 hours

Culture

Mandalay

The last royal capital, and still the country's cultural centre for musicians, weavers and gold-leaf makers. The palace, the monasteries around it and the teak bridge at U Bein are the obvious stops; the workshops are the interesting ones.

Mandalay Region · on the Irrawaddy · allow 2 days

Coast

Ngapali Beach

A curved bay of pale sand and palm trees on the Rakhine coast, with fishing villages at either end and very little development between them. The most straightforward place in the country to do nothing at all.

Rakhine State · seasonal flights from Yangon · allow 3–5 days

Landscape

Hpa-An

Limestone karsts rising out of rice fields in Kayin State, riddled with caves containing Buddha images and reached by boat, bicycle or a very hot climb. A good counterweight to the temple trail.

Kayin State · 6 hours from Yangon · allow 2 days

Planning

When to go

Myanmar has three seasons, and they behave very differently depending on whether you are on the coast or in the dry zone.

November – February

The best window. Cool, dry and clear across most of the country. Bagan's mornings are cold enough for a jacket, and the coast is at its most reliable.

March – May

Hot, and then very hot. Temperatures in the dry zone pass 40°C before Thingyan in mid-April. Good for the coast, hard work for temple-hopping.

June – October

Monsoon. Cheaper, greener and emptier, with short heavy downpours. Some coastal resorts and trekking routes close entirely.

Practical

Things worth knowing

  • Some places are off limits. Access changes with the fighting. Large parts of Rakhine, Kachin, Kayah and Sagaing states are effectively closed to travellers, and permits are needed for others.
  • Domestic flights beat roads. Where flights operate, they are far quicker and safer than long-distance buses on poor roads.
  • Carry cash. Kyat is required almost everywhere; cards work in bigger hotels and little else. Bring clean, unmarked foreign notes to exchange.
  • Dress for the temples. Shoulders and knees covered, shoes and socks off. A light longyi is the most practical thing you can pack.
  • Connectivity is patchy. Mobile data is cheap but slow outside the cities, and the authorities have cut internet access in conflict areas before.
  • Tipping is not expected but is appreciated, particularly by guides and drivers whose incomes collapsed during the pandemic and the coup.

Check before you book

Tourism in Myanmar is contested: some Burmese organisations argue that any travel funds the military, while others depend on visitor income. Whatever you decide, check your government's current advisory and read the full travel advisory on this site first.
